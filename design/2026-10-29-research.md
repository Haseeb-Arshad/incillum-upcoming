# The case in figures: research report

Prepared 29 October 2026 for the front page and for the funding application.

This file records every figure the site prints that is not the example
quotation's own, where each one comes from, how far it was checked, what it can
and cannot be used to say, and the figures that were looked at and turned down.

The rule it serves is simple. Incillum is pre-launch. It has no customers, no
usage and nothing measured, so it prints no figure about itself (AGENTS.md §5).
Every figure on the page is a third party's, about the work, with the source
printed next to it. A buyer or an investor who checks one should find it exactly
where we said it was.

## 1. How fast companies answer, and what waiting costs

**Source.** James B. Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington, "The Short
Life of Online Sales Leads", _Harvard Business Review_, March 2011, p. 28 (HBR
Reprint F1103B). https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads

**What it measured.** Two studies.

1. An audit of **2,241 US companies**, each sent a web-generated test enquiry,
   timing the reply. Results: 37% replied within an hour, 16% within one to 24
   hours, 24% took more than 24 hours, and **23% never replied**. Among
   companies that replied within 30 days, **the average reply took 42 hours**.
2. A separate study of **1.25 million sales leads** received by 29 B2C and 13
   B2B companies in the US. Firms that tried to contact a potential customer
   within an hour of the query were **nearly seven times as likely to qualify
   the lead** as firms that tried even an hour later, and **more than 60 times
   as likely** as firms that waited 24 hours or longer. "Qualify" was defined as
   having a meaningful conversation with a key decision maker.

**What we can say with it.**

- "The average company took 42 hours to answer an enquiry. Nearly a quarter
  never answered at all."
- "Answer within the hour and you are nearly seven times as likely to reach the
  person who decides as if you answer an hour later. Wait a day and it is more
  than sixty times."

**What we cannot say with it.**

- That it measured requests for quotation. It measured web enquiries, mostly
  in consumer-facing industries (insurance, cars, lending, education). The
  mechanism, interest going cold while a request waits, is the same one an RFQ
  is exposed to, and the page says "enquiry", not "RFQ", when it quotes it.
- That 7× is exact. The authors say "nearly seven times". The page prints 7×
  with "nearly" in the sentence beside it.
- That it is current. It is from 2011. It is still the most cited peer-reviewed
  measurement of response speed, and nothing since has shown companies getting
  much faster, but the year is printed with it.

**Caveat worth knowing.** David Elkington was chairman and CEO of
InsideSales.com, which sold software for exactly this problem. The study was
published by HBR with a Harvard Business School co-author, which is why it is
used, but an investor may raise it, and it is better to have said so first.

**Why it is on the page.** It is the cost of waiting in one number a buyer
remembers. Incillum's whole argument is the hours when nobody is at the desk,
and this is what those hours are worth.

## 2. Where a sales rep's week goes

**Source.** Salesforce, _State of Sales_, seventh edition. Anonymous survey of
**4,050 sales professionals in 22 countries**, fielded August to September
2025, all respondents third-party panellists.
https://www.salesforce.com/en-us/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/documents/reports/sales/salesforce-state-of-sales-report-2026.pdf

**The chart (page 8, "How reps spend their time during an average workweek").**

| Activity               | Share   | Salesforce's grouping |
| ---------------------- | ------- | --------------------- |
| Meeting with customers | 22%     | Selling               |
| Prospecting            | 18%     | Selling               |
| Creating quotes        | 17%     | Not selling           |
| Planning               | 16%     | Not selling           |
| Manually entering data | 13%     | Not selling           |
| Training               | 11%     | Not selling           |
| Other                  | 3%      | Not selling           |
| **Selling**            | **40%** |                       |
| **Not selling**        | **60%** |                       |

**What we can say with it.**

- "Reps spend 17% of their week creating quotes, nearly as much as the 22% they
  spend with customers."
- "Creating quotes and entering data by hand take 30% of the week."
- "Sixty per cent of a rep's week is not selling."

**What we cannot say with it.**

- That Incillum gives that 30% back. Nothing has been measured. The page says
  this is the work Charlie is built for, not how much of it disappears.

**A note on the report's own words.** The text on the same page says reps spend
"more than half of their time on nonselling work like data entry and
prospecting", while the chart counts prospecting as selling. The page uses the
chart's numbers, which add up, and does not use the sentence.

**The previous edition.** The sixth edition (published 25 July 2024, 5,500
sales professionals in 27 countries) reported that reps spend **70%** of their
time on non-selling tasks. It is a more dramatic number and it is real, but it
is older and the seventh edition replaces it, so the page uses the seventh.
https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/sales-ai-statistics-2024/

**Caveat.** Salesforce sells sales software and AI agents. The time-use chart
is a plain survey result and is widely used, but the report's statements about
how much agents help (for example "94% of sales leaders with agents say they're
critical") are the vendor talking about its own category and are not used.

## 3. What one point of price is worth

**Source.** Michael V. Marn, Eric V. Roegner and Craig C. Zawada, "The power of
pricing", _McKinsey Quarterly_, 2003 number 1, pp. 27 to 36. Exhibit 1, "The
power of one", source Compustat and McKinsey analysis.
https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-power-of-pricing

**What it says (p. 29).** For the average income statement of an S&P 1500
company, a price rise of 1 percent, if volumes remained stable, would generate
an **8 percent increase in operating profit**. That is nearly 50% more than the
effect of a 1% fall in variable costs, and more than three times the effect of a
1% rise in volume. It cuts both ways: a 1% fall in average price takes operating
profit down by the same 8%.

**What we can say with it.**

- "For the average large company, a price 1% higher at the same volume lifts
  operating profit by about 8%. It works the other way too."

**What we cannot say with it.**

- That every business sees 8%. It is an average for large listed US companies;
  a distributor on thin margins sees a larger effect, a software company a
  smaller one. The page says "the average large company".
- The often-quoted "8.7%" or "11%". 8.7% appears only in secondary sources; 11%
  is from an earlier McKinsey sample (Marn and Rosiello, HBR, 1992). The page
  uses the 8% the 2003 article prints.

**Why it is on the page.** Margin is the second thing a quotation can lose
while it waits, after the customer. The example on the page, line 14 of RFQ
8841, is the same arithmetic at the scale of one line: a supplier cost up 6% at
03:42, which, missed, sends the line out EUR 11,520.00 below the agreed floor.
The page reads that figure from the evidence document rather than writing it
again, so it cannot drift from the rest of the example.

## 4. Why now: agents are arriving, and most will not last

**Source A.** Gartner, Top Strategic Technology Trends for 2025, October 2024:
by 2028, **33% of enterprise software applications will include agentic AI, up
from less than 1% in 2024**, enabling at least 15% of day-to-day work decisions
to be made by agents. Reported identically by Reuters and others; Gartner's own
article is at https://www.gartner.com/en/articles/ai-agents-pc1 .

**Source B.** Gartner press release, 25 June 2025, "Gartner Predicts Over 40% of
Agentic AI Projects Will Be Canceled by End of 2027", citing escalating costs,
unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. The same release warns of
"agent washing" and estimates that only about 130 of the thousands of vendors
claiming agentic products are real.
https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027

**What we can say with them.**

- "In 2024, fewer than one in a hundred enterprise applications had an AI agent
  in it. Gartner expects one in three by 2028."
- "Gartner also expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by
  the end of 2027, over cost, unclear value or weak risk controls."

**Why the second one is on a sales page.** It is the buyer's real fear, said by
somebody they trust. A commercial director has seen software promise and fail.
Saying first that most agent projects will be cancelled, and why, is what lets
the page say what Incillum does differently: every figure cited, the arithmetic
exact, nothing sent without a person. It is also the strongest single argument
to an investor that the category needs a careful product, not a fast demo.

**Verification still owed.** Open both Gartner pages in a browser and confirm
the wording before launch. Do not use the word "autonomously" from Source A on
the page; the site never describes Charlie that way.

## 5. The hours

Not a statistic, but a fact a reader can check: when it is 03:42 in central
Europe in late October (CET, UTC+1, after the clocks change on 25 October
2026), it is 02:42 UTC and **10:42 in China** (UTC+8). The night thread's
supplier answers at 03:42; where it was written, it was mid-morning.

The world map on the page is drawn at that minute. The sun is overhead at about
13.5°S (solar declination in late October) and about 140°E (02:42 UTC, plus the
equation of time, which runs about a quarter of an hour fast at the end of
October). Europe and Africa are dark; East and South Asia and Australia are in
daylight. The land is Natural Earth's 1:110m land (public domain), sampled onto
a dot grid by `design/generate-world.mjs`.

## 7. Looked at and turned down

Each of these appears on many vendor blogs. None is used, because none has a
source a buyer could check.

- "35 to 50% of sales go to the vendor that responds first." Attributed to
  InsideSales.com; no study behind it could be found.
- "Manufacturers that answer an RFQ within 4 hours win 35 to 50% more deals"
  (Optifai, citing an "Industrial Distribution 2024 Benchmark" that could not be
  found).
- "Average manufacturing quote turnaround is 5.6 days" (Docket, citing Mavlon).
  A vendor's analysis of its own market.
- "Responding first raises win rates 50%" (Broadn). No sample given.
- "A 75-person components maker cut response time from 52 to 8 hours and won
  41% more." One vendor's case study.
- "The odds of contacting a lead fall 100 times if you wait 30 minutes instead
  of 5." From the 2007 Lead Response Management study by the same InsideSales
  team; real, but unreviewed and about phone contact, and the HBR article makes
  the same point with a reviewed method.
- "Gartner: 77% of B2B buyers say their last purchase was very complex or
  difficult." Plausible and often quoted, but not traced to a primary source in
  this research.

## 8. How the figures are held true

- They live in `figures` in `src/content/site.ts`, outside `glass`, whose own
  rule is that it writes no figure.
- `src/content/site.test.ts` checks that every figure carries its source, that
  no figure is about Incillum, that the week's shares are the survey's and add
  up, and that the map is drawn at the night thread's own hours.
- `e2e/glass.spec.ts` checks that every figure on the rendered page has its
  source beside it, and that the bar is drawn from the shares it prints.
- If a source changes or a newer edition replaces it, change the figure, the
  source line and this file in the same commit.
